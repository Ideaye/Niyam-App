This recommendation is withdrawn.

The wedge it rests on — pronunciation feedback as the thing YouTube cannot do — already exists. Vāgbodhinī, from Dr. Prathosh A.P.'s lab at IISc Bengaluru, is a public-beta tutor doing syllable-by-syllable recitation scoring, backed by a purpose-built Sanskrit speech stack that is open-sourced with published weights. Slow-playback practice apps (Srimad Gita, Memorize Shloka, Sloka Saagar) already ship the listen-and-repeat feature free. Read devotional-tech-ad-test.html instead. Kept unedited below as a record of the reasoning that was wrong.

The answer

A shloka tutor for diaspora Hindu families — parent-bought, child-used, pronunciation-first.

The one thing a diaspora parent cannot give their child themselves and cannot get from YouTube: the ability to recite correctly, and to know it is correct. Everything else in this category is either taken, ops-heavy, or doesn't monetise.

My confidence this is the right pick: ~55–60%. Not high. Read §5 before acting.

Section 1How everything else got eliminated

Three hard constraints do most of the work. You are one non-coding founder with LLM agents, no ops function, no capital, and — evidenced by the unresolved native-speaker review backlog in forlater.md — content authenticity is already your bottleneck, not code.

The decisive new finding

Both documented deaths in Indian faith tech were ops-heavy supply-side aggregators. My Tirth India (temples + priests + hotels + travel agents) shut in Aug 2024 on a funding crisis. DevDham — which I wrongly listed as alive in yesterday's pack — shut down after founder exits, having raised ~$1M and claimed 500+ temples and 2,000 pandits.

Sri Mandir survives that same model only because it raised $53M and built its own fulfilment hubs in 20 temple towns. Anything requiring a supply side is off the table for you.

Section 2Why this one survives

The buyer is the best-evidenced payer in the entire category

  • ~5.9M Hindus across US (3.37M), UK (1.07M), Canada (828k), Australia (684k) [A]
  • Indian American median household income: $151,200 (2023) — roughly double the US median [A, Pew]
  • Sri Mandir already extracts ₹7,000/yr ARPU from this cohort vs ₹600–800 in India — ~10x [B]
  • Prayer-to-payment conversion is ~50% in the US vs 20–25% in India [B]

The demand is proven by revealed behaviour, not by trend pieces

Chinmaya Mission's Bala Vihar — weekend dharma classes for children — grew in the Bay Area chapter alone from 6 children in 1981 to over 2,150 today, running Sept–June with paid language classes alongside. Diaspora parents demonstrably give up their weekends and their money for this. That is a far stronger signal than any "Gen Z is turning spiritual" article in the research pack.

You already own the hard part of the content

Niyam has ~208 mantras with verified transliterations, authored meanings, and UI across 8 languages. For a shloka-teaching product that is the core corpus. Unlike a festival product — where Gujarati, Tamil, Bengali and Marwari households do genuinely different things and getting it wrong is offensive — a shloka is a shloka. The content forks far less by region, which is what makes it tractable for a team of one.

The wedge YouTube cannot copy

YouTube has infinite "learn the Hanuman Chalisa" videos and they are free. What no video can do is tell a specific child whether they just said it correctly. A diaspora parent who does not read Devanagari has no way to correct their own child. That is the sharpest, most specific unmet need I found anywhere in this research, and it is a software problem rather than an ops problem.

Section 3What the product actually is

  • Account holder is the parent. An adult buys, manages, and sees progress. Children use it in a mode the parent opens. No child accounts, no child-collected data, no social layer, no ads.
  • Core loop: hear a reference recitation → see the line in Devanagari + transliteration + meaning → recite it → get feedback → repeat until it holds.
  • Progression: line by line, then verse, then whole text. Short daily units, not lessons.
  • Meaning is first-class, not an appendix. The value proposition to a diaspora parent is "my child knows what they are saying" — that is the thing they most fear losing.
  • Positioned as complement to Bala Vihar, never as replacement. Bala Vihar meets once a week. This covers the other six days. Framing it as a substitute picks a fight with an institution you cannot beat and do not want to.
  • Priced at real diaspora price points — think $6–10/month or $60–80/year, not ₹99. The entire thesis collapses if you price it for India.

Section 5Deep audit — where this breaks

Ordered by how likely each is to kill the thing. The first two are serious enough that I would not write a line of code before resolving them.

1. Sanskrit pronunciation scoring may simply not workcould kill it

This is the feature the entire differentiation rests on, and I am genuinely uncertain it is achievable. Off-the-shelf speech recognition (Whisper, Google STT, Apple's on-device models) is not trained on Sanskrit recitation. Sanskrit has phonemic distinctions — retroflex vs dental, aspirated vs unaspirated, long vs short vowels, visarga — that general ASR collapses or ignores. Scoring a child's Sanskrit, with a child's vocal tract and an accent shaped by English, is harder still.

Doing this properly is likely forced phoneme alignment against a reference recitation with a custom or fine-tuned acoustic model — closer to a research project than an API call. The honest fallback is much cruder: did they attempt the whole line, roughly in time, roughly in the right shape. That fallback may not be worth paying for.

Resolve first Spend a week, before anything else, testing whether any available model can reliably distinguish a correct recitation from a subtly wrong one on ten Chalisa lines. If it cannot, this product loses its wedge and the recommendation changes.

2. It drags you into child-directed app regulation you deliberately avoidedcould kill it

You just spent a Play Console session deliberately marking Niyam 18+ and unchecking 16–17 to stay out of DPDP and Families obligations. This product walks straight back in. A shloka app for children is child-directed by appeal, which is what Play's Families policy actually tests — not merely who holds the account. That means: US COPPA, UK Age Appropriate Design Code, India DPDP verifiable parental consent, Google Play Families policy review, and a materially stricter bar on ads, IAP presentation, and data.

It is survivable — no ads, no child accounts, no analytics on children, parent-gated purchase — but it is real, recurring compliance work, and it is the exact burden you decided against three months ago.

Decide explicitly This is a founder call, not a technical one. If the answer is "not willing", this recommendation is void and the runner-up in §7 takes over.

3. Content authenticity is already your known weak pointhigh

forlater.md item #1 has been open since June: 208 machine-generated meaning blocks across 7 languages still awaiting native-speaker review, because no reviewers were available. This product multiplies that obligation and raises the stakes — an inaccurate meaning taught to an adult is embarrassing; taught to a child as authoritative, it is something else. And you would need recitation audio of unimpeachable quality, which cannot be generated.

Implication You need at least one credentialed human — a Sanskrit teacher or pandit — attached to this product before launch, not after. That is a real dependency you do not currently have, and it partially reintroduces a supply side.

4. Two audiences, one product — the classic kids-app traphigh

The parent pays; the child decides whether it survives. Children's education apps have notoriously brutal retention — the parent subscribes in a burst of intention and the child abandons it in three weeks. You must satisfy an adult's sense of authenticity and a nine-year-old's tolerance for boredom, and those two pull in opposite directions. Making it fun risks the parent judging it irreverent; making it reverent risks the child quitting.

Watch for If early testing shows children need gamification to stay, and parents recoil at gamified scripture, the product has no viable centre.

5. You throw away your only genuinely hard technical assethigh

Niyam's accessibility-service engine — launch interception, overlay, interval check-in, service survival across OEM battery killers — is difficult, working, tested, and shipped. None of it transfers. Only the mantra corpus does. You would be restarting on the hardest part of the engineering while abandoning the part you already won.

Counterweight This is the strongest argument for §6 instead of a new app.

6. Bala Vihar is a beloved incumbent, not an absent onemedium

It is free or near-free, in-person, socially embedded, and run by people the parents already trust. You cannot beat it. You can only be the six days it isn't. That caps the product's ambition and means your best distribution channel is also your competitor — which makes partnership awkward.

7. Distribution requires community work you may not want to domedium

There is no cheap paid channel to diaspora Hindu parents. The real channels are temples, Bala Vihar chapters, WhatsApp groups, and festival moments — all of which require showing up as a person, in communities, repeatedly. That is founder-time-intensive and unglamorous, and it is a different job from the one you have been doing.

8. Diaspora willingness-to-pay is inferred, not testedmedium

The ₹7,000 ARPU figure is Sri Mandir's, for ritual transactions, reported by the company to a journalist. It is not evidence that the same people will pay a recurring subscription for education software. I am extrapolating across both product category and payment model. That extrapolation is the foundation of the whole recommendation and it is untested.

Which is why §6 exists.

Section 6The cheaper thing to do first

Strong advice

Before building anything new, test the diaspora thesis using Niyam, which already exists. Niyam already ships worldwide through Play. Set diaspora-appropriate pricing, point a landing page at Hindu parents in the US/UK/Canada, and see whether that audience converts and pays at multiples of the Indian rate.

This costs days, not months. And it tests risk #8 — the single assumption the entire new-app recommendation rests on — using an asset you have already built and shipped. If diaspora Hindus won't pay a premium for your product, the shloka tutor's business case evaporates before you have spent anything on it. If they will, you have both a validated thesis and a revenue base to build from.

Section 7If the answer to risk #2 is "no"

If you are not willing to re-enter child-directed app regulation, the recommendation changes to the runner-up: the same product, aimed at adults instead of children — diaspora adults who never learned, or have forgotten, and are quietly embarrassed about it. Same corpus, same pronunciation mechanic, same price point, no COPPA, no Families policy, no two-audience trap.

It is a weaker business — the emotional urgency of "my child will lose this" is far stronger than "I should know this" — but it is meaningfully simpler and it removes the two kill-risks at once. If risk #1 also fails, then I would not build a new app at all, and §6 becomes the whole answer.

Section 8What would change my mind

  • Pronunciation scoring proves unworkable in a week of testing → recommendation void (§7 or §6).
  • You are unwilling to take on child-directed compliance → drop to the adult version.
  • Diaspora pricing test on Niyam shows no premium willingness-to-pay → the whole diaspora thesis fails and I would go back to Niyam's own attention wedge.
  • A credentialed Sanskrit reviewer cannot be secured → do not ship; the authenticity risk is not survivable in a devotional product.
  • Evidence surfaces that an existing app already does pronunciation feedback well → re-run §1 entirely.

Section 9What I need from you

  1. Risk #2 — are you willing to build a child-directed app and take on COPPA / UK AADC / DPDP / Play Families? Yes or no changes the answer.
  2. Do you want me to run the pronunciation-scoring feasibility test (risk #1) before anything else? It is the cheapest way to find out if this is real.
  3. Do you want the §6 diaspora pricing test on Niyam regardless of which app you choose? I think you should, and it is nearly free.

None of these should be answered from the deck. All three are cheap to resolve empirically, and I have deliberately not started any of them without your call.

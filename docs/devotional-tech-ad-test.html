Section 0 — added 2026-08-29The actual situation, and what follows from it

You have a finished product — v1.0.6, signed AAB built, backend live, billing wired, eight languages, 125 tests passing — that has never been published. And the last four days have been spent asking which new app to build.

The point

Every question in this whole thread — will the diaspora pay, is there real demand, what is the ARPU, does anyone want a pause before the scroll — is answerable for free by launching the thing that is already built, and answerable no other way for less than several hundred dollars and several weeks of proxy measurement.

An unlaunched app generates exactly zero information. A launched one generates the entire dataset the ad test was trying to approximate: real installs, by country, with real retention and real paid conversion, continuously, at no media cost.

The gate that may sit between you and production

If your Play developer account is a personal account created after 13 November 2023, Google requires a closed test with at least 12 testers, opted in continuously for 14 days, before you can even apply for production access — and since 2026 it also checks the testers genuinely used the app. Organization accounts, and personal accounts opened before that date, are exempt and can publish straight to production.

Source quality note: the policy is real and well documented, but most of what is written about it online comes from companies that sell tester recruitment, so treat the finer details as C and confirm against Play Console itself.

Which account you have is the single fact that sets your timeline, and I do not know it. If the gate applies, the clock does not start until the AAB is uploaded — so every day it sits on your Desktop is a day added to when you can sell anything at all.

That is, feature for feature, the product you described.

And the differentiator I proposed is also taken

Vāgbodhinī — from Dr. Prathosh A.P.'s lab at IISc Bengaluru — is a public-beta tutor that combines chant playback with syllable-by-syllable scoring, verifying recitation against the known text. That is precisely the wedge I said YouTube could not copy.

It sits on a purpose-built Sanskrit speech stack, all released:

  • Vāgdhenu — metre-aware TTS for chanted recitation, expert rating ~4.6/5, weights on GitHub and Hugging Face with a live demo
  • Su-śrotā — Sanskrit recogniser (IndicConformer-CTC), word error 86% → 34%, character error 24% → 16%, open-source with a live tool

Two consumer apps already ship from it: Bhāgavata-VāNi (web, iOS, Android, free) and Vedavani (iOS, Android). The recogniser improves off consented user data — so it compounds.

Where I was wrong, in both directions

I said Sanskrit pronunciation scoring might not be achievable. I was too pessimistic on the technology and far too optimistic on the opportunity. Scoring against a known text is forced alignment, not open-vocabulary recognition — a much easier problem than the 62.3% phoneme-accuracy figure I was reasoning from. It works well enough. But that no longer helps us: it has been built, by people with Sanskrit-speech credentials we cannot assemble, and given away with the model weights. A moat that is open-source is not a moat.

Recommendation withdrawn

The shloka tutor is dead as a business. devotional-tech-recommendation.html is marked superseded and kept unedited as a record.

Section 2Do this before spending a rupee

Free · today · possibly conclusive

You may already own the answer. Check Play Console first.

Niyam has been shipping worldwide through Google Play. Play Console already reports installs, retention, and revenue broken down by country. Before designing any experiment, pull that: how many US, UK, Canada and Australia installs do you have, what do they retain like against India, and — if any bought — what did they pay?

The entire diaspora thesis rests on one claim: these users pay multiples of Indian users. You may have months of real evidence for or against it sitting in a dashboard, for free, with no ad spend and no landing page. Do this first. An ad test that contradicts your own live data would be worthless anyway.

Second free source: your existing users. You have their emails through Supabase auth. A three-question survey to your international users beats cold traffic on anything about motivation, and costs nothing. (Sending it is your call — I won't email anyone on your behalf.)

Section 3The constraint that will break a naive ad test

You cannot target Hindus. On either platform.

Meta: from 19 January 2022, detailed targeting options referencing religion were removed outright — you cannot target religious practices, groups, or observances. Enforcement has since tightened; as of 15 January 2025 even religion-related keyword targeting is blocked.

Google: policy prohibits selecting or targeting personalised ads, or using audience data, based on religious belief. Religious belief is a sensitive interest category — you may use predefined Google audiences but not advertiser-curated ones. Ads touching it get flagged "Religious belief in personalized advertising" and run with targeting restrictions applied.

So "run an ad to Hindus in the US, UK and India" is not a thing you can buy. What you can buy:

RouteStrengthNotes
Lookalike from your own Niyam user listBest availableYour own first-party data, uploaded as a custom audience. This is the one route the policy leaves fully open, and you already have the asset.
GeographicStrongRadius targeting on dense diaspora areas — Edison & Jersey City NJ, Fremont & Sunnyvale CA, Harrow & Wembley & Leicester UK, Brampton ON. Legal, precise, cheap.
LanguageModerateHindi / Gujarati / Tamil speakers. Language is not a protected category.
YouTube placementModeratePlacing on specific devotional channels is placement targeting, not audience targeting — a different policy surface. Verify before relying on it.
Interest proxies (Bollywood, cricket, Indian cuisine)WeakVery leaky. Use only to broaden reach, never as the primary cut.

Also expect creative-level friction. Ad copy naming deities, mantras or worship may itself trip the religious-belief flag on Google. Budget time for disapprovals and rewrites; do not schedule this against a hard deadline.

Section 4Track A — the test worth running

You have something almost nobody running a smoke test has: a real, shipped, purchasable product. So don't fake-door it. Sell it, and measure money.

Design

Two geographies, real installs, real prices, real conversions. Identical creative and identical landing experience; the only deliberate variable is country and price point.

CellGeoPriceTargeting
A1India (tier-1 metros)Current India pricingGeo + language + lookalike
A2US — diaspora-dense metros3–5x the India priceGeo radius + lookalike
A3UK — London + Leicester3–5x the India priceGeo radius + lookalike

The metric that matters

Not clicks. Not installs. Paid conversion rate per install, and revenue per install, by geo. Clicks measure curiosity; the entire research pack shows this category drowns in curiosity that never converts — 40M downloads against 3.5M monthly actives.

Decide the thresholds before you spend, not after

  • Thesis confirmed: US/UK revenue per install ≥ 3x India's, at ≥3x the price. Diaspora is your market; reprice and reposition immediately.
  • Ambiguous: 1.5–3x. Real but not transformative. Worth a second, larger round before acting.
  • Thesis dead: <1.5x. The 10x ARPU figure does not transfer to your product, and every diaspora-based plan in these documents loses its foundation. That is a genuinely valuable finding — it would save you a year.

Budget

Start at $300–600 total, weighted toward the US and UK cells because clicks there cost far more. Run 10–14 days minimum so weekday/weekend effects wash out.

I am not confident about current CPCs in these segments and I have not researched them — treat the figure as a starting stake, not a forecast. Set a hard daily cap and check spend on day two.

Section 5Track B — only if you still want a new-concept read

If you want to test a concept that doesn't exist yet, the honest version is a landing page with a stated price and an email capture, where the "Subscribe" button leads immediately to "not live yet — join the list". No money is taken for a product that does not exist. That line is not negotiable, and it's also what keeps you the right side of app-store and ad-platform policy.

But be clear about what it can tell you. A click on a promise costs nothing, so it measures appeal, not demand. And for the shloka tutor specifically, the result is now uninterpretable — interest would be confounded with the free incumbents already serving that need. If you want Track B, point it at a proposition we have not already found an incumbent for.
